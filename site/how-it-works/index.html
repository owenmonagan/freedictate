How it works

How FreeDictate works

Press your button, talk, let go. Here is every step between your voice and the text, and all of it runs on your Mac.

FreeDictate is offline speech to text for Mac. There is no cloud service behind it. Five small, well-known pieces of software work together on your computer: Hammerspoon listens for your button, ffmpeg records your voice, whisper.cpp turns speech into text, and macOS pastes the result. This page walks through each step in order.

The whole trip, in six steps

  1. You press your talk key. Hammerspoon notices and FreeDictate starts listening.
  2. ffmpeg records your microphone as 16 kHz mono audio to a temporary file, and reports your voice level so the pill can show a live meter.
  3. You let go (or tap again). Recording stops.
  4. The audio goes to whisper-server, a whisper.cpp speech engine running on your own Mac at 127.0.0.1:8178.
  5. Text comes back. If the server isn't answering, FreeDictate runs whisper-cli instead.
  6. The text is pasted into the app you were using with ⌘V, and your clipboard is put back the way it was.

Hammerspoon: the part that listens

Hammerspoon is a free, open-source Mac automation tool. FreeDictate is a single Hammerspoon module, app/dictate.lua. The installer links it to ~/.hammerspoon/freedictate.lua and adds one line, require("freedictate").start(), to your Hammerspoon config. That is the whole app. It adds a FreeDictate icon to the menu bar, where you can change your talk key or reopen Setup.

Your talk key

The talk key can be almost anything: a single key, a combo, a lone modifier such as Right Option, or the middle, back or forward mouse button. The default is ⌥ Space. There are two ways to use it, and you don't have to choose between them:

  • Hold the key, talk, and release. Recording stops when you let go.
  • Tap the key, talk hands-free, and tap again to stop.

FreeDictate tells them apart by timing. A press shorter than 0.35 seconds is a tap. Anything longer is a hold. Press Esc while listening to cancel, and nothing is pasted.

Recording with ffmpeg

ffmpeg records from your microphone straight to a WAV file in your temporary folder, $TMPDIR/freedictate.wav. It records at 16 kHz in mono, which is the format whisper models expect, so nothing needs converting later. Each new recording replaces the last one.

While it records, ffmpeg's astats filter measures how loud you are, about 15 times a second. FreeDictate sends that level to the pill on screen, so you can see it is hearing you.

The speech engine: whisper.cpp on your Mac

Transcription uses whisper.cpp, a fast C++ version of OpenAI's Whisper speech recognition model that runs entirely on your computer. FreeDictate uses the ggml-large-v3-turbo-q5_0 model, a compressed build of Whisper large-v3 turbo that balances accuracy and speed. The installer downloads it once, about 550 MB, to ~/.local/share/whisper/.

whisper-server, always ready

Loading a large model takes time, so FreeDictate keeps it loaded. The installer creates a LaunchAgent named dev.freedictate.whisper-server that runs whisper-server in the background, listening only on 127.0.0.1:8178. That address is your own Mac. Nothing else on your network can reach it. macOS starts it at login and restarts it if it stops. It is set to English.

When you finish talking, FreeDictate posts the WAV file to the local server and gets plain text back. Because the model is already in memory, the text is usually ready a moment after you stop.

whisper-cli, the fallback

If the server is down or returns an error, FreeDictate doesn't give up. It runs whisper-cli on the same file with the same model. That is slower, because the model has to load first, but you still get your words.

Pasting, without losing your clipboard

FreeDictate saves whatever is on your clipboard, puts the transcript there, presses ⌘V for you, and then restores what you had. The words land in whatever app has focus: your editor, a chat box, an email, a terminal. Pasting needs Accessibility access for Hammerspoon. If it isn't granted, the text stays on your clipboard and the pill tells you to press ⌘V yourself.

The pill on your screen

A small dark pill at the bottom of the screen shows what is happening: listening, with the live level meter in red; transcribing; and a brief confirmation when the text is pasted. It is a transparent web view drawn by Hammerspoon, 40 pixels tall, and it gets out of the way as soon as it is done. It respects Reduce Motion in System Settings.

Setup, step by step

The first time FreeDictate runs, a Setup window walks you through everything it needs, one step at a time:

  1. Welcome.
  2. Microphone permission for Hammerspoon.
  3. Accessibility permission, so it can paste.
  4. Speech engine, a check that whisper-server is answering.
  5. Start at login, so your button works after a restart.
  6. Your button, where you press the key or mouse button you want.
  7. Try it, a live test: talk and see your words appear.

You can reopen Setup any time from the menu bar with Setup….

Why it is built this way

Every piece is free, open source and already trusted by many Mac users. Nothing is hidden in a compiled app, and there is nothing to subscribe to. The code that does all of this is one Lua file you can read in an afternoon. See the source on GitHub, or read the privacy page for what stays on your Mac.
