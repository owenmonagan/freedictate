Push to talk dictation on Mac

Hold a key, talk, let go. Your words are pasted into whatever app you're using. No mode switching, no window to manage.

Push to talk is the simplest way to control dictation. The microphone is on while you hold the key and off when you let go. You never wonder whether it's still listening, and it never picks up a conversation you didn't mean to dictate.

FreeDictate is built around this one gesture. Hold your button and talk. Let go, and the text is pasted into the app with focus. It's free, open source and offline.

How push to talk works in FreeDictate

Do thisWhat happens
Hold your button, talk, let goRecording stops when you release. Your words are pasted.
Tap your button, talk, tap againHands-free. A press shorter than 0.35 seconds counts as a tap.
Press Esc while listeningCancels. Nothing is pasted.

The same button does both. FreeDictate decides by how long you hold it. A press longer than 0.35 seconds is a hold, so releasing it stops recording. A shorter press is a tap, which starts hands-free recording until you tap again. You don't have to switch modes.

When recording stops, FreeDictate sends the audio to the speech engine on your Mac, then pastes the text with ⌘V. It restores whatever was on your clipboard afterwards, so dictation doesn't wipe out something you copied.

Set it up

  1. Install. Run the installer from Terminal: 
    git clone https://github.com/owenmonagan/freedictate.git
cd freedictate
./install.sh
  2. Allow the microphone and Accessibility for Hammerspoon when Setup asks. Accessibility is needed to paste into other apps.
  3. Pick your talk key on the "Your button" step. The default is ⌥ Space.
  4. Try it in the live test at the end of Setup.

To change the key later, click the FreeDictate menu bar icon and choose Set talk key…, then press the key, combo or mouse button you want. Reset to ⌥Space puts the default back.

Choosing a good push to talk key

You can use any key, any combo, a lone modifier, or the middle, back or forward mouse button. A few things make one choice better than another.

A lone modifier

Right Option or Right Command are popular. They sit under your thumb, and you rarely press them on their own. Holding one feels like holding a walkie-talkie button.

A combo

The default, ⌥ Space, is easy to reach and rarely used by other apps. Combos are a safe pick if a lone modifier clashes with your own shortcuts.

A function key

An unused F-key works, as long as your Mac sends it as a normal key rather than a media control. Check the keyboard settings in System Settings if it doesn't register.

A mouse button

If your hand lives on the mouse, a side button is hard to beat. See mouse button dictation.

Avoid keys you type with.

A letter or Space on its own would stop doing its normal job. Pick something you don't need mid-sentence.

Hold or tap?

Hold for short things: a search query, a Slack reply, a file name. Your finger tells you when it's recording, and letting go is the "send".

Tap for longer things: an email, notes, a long prompt. Tap once, talk for as long as you need with your hands free, then tap again. If you change your mind, press Esc and nothing is pasted.

Tips for clean results

  • Start talking right after you press. Recording begins on the press, so a short pause before you speak is fine, but long silences just add time.
  • Speak in full sentences. Whisper adds punctuation from how you phrase things, so natural speech beats word-by-word dictation.
  • Watch the meter. The pill at the bottom of the screen shows a live level. If it doesn't move, the mic isn't hearing you.
  • Click into the field first. Text goes to whichever app has focus when the paste happens.

Why push to talk instead of always listening

Always-on dictation has to guess when you're talking to it. Push to talk doesn't guess. It also means the microphone records only while you ask it to, and audio is processed by whisper.cpp on your own Mac. Nothing is sent anywhere.
